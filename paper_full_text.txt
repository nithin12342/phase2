[4][Normal] Abstract—Depression is a prevalent mental health disorder affecting approximately 280 million people globally, contributing to an estimated $1 trillion annual economic burden through lost productivity and healthcare costs. Traditional diagnostic methods, primarily the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-8), rely heavily on subjective self-reporting and are susceptible to recall bias, social desirability effects, and the Hawthorne phenomenon. This paper presents H⁵-OmniFusion, a novel five-modality hierarchical fusion architecture for automated depression detection that addresses fundamental limitations of existing unimodal and multimodal approaches. The proposed framework integrates Audio (A), Text (T), Video (V), Facial (F), and Tabular (B) modalities through state-of-the-art deep learning backbones: Wav2Vec2-Large-XLSR-53 combined with eGeMAPSv02 for acoustic analysis achieving 84% baseline accuracy, MentalRoBERTa pre-trained on mental health forums achieving 91% accuracy, VideoMAE-Base employing masked autoencoding for temporal video understanding at 76-79% accuracy, OpenFace 2.0 integrated with POSTER v2 for facial action unit and expression analysis at 79% accuracy, and TabPFN leveraging prior-data fitted networks for tabular metadata processing at 85% accuracy. The architecture introduces a novel three-level hypergraph fusion mechanism: (1) Local Hypergraph for intra-modality temporal dependencies using hyperedge message passing, (2) Modality Hypergraph enabling cross-modal attention through learned hyperedge connections, and (3) Latent Perceiver for global context aggregation via learned latent queries. A quality-aware Mixture of Experts (MoE) layer with Top-K=3 routing enables dynamic modality weighting based on input signal quality, providing robustness to noisy or missing modalities. The multi-task learning formulation optimizes a combined objective comprising Focal Loss for binary classification, Mean Squared Error for PHQ-8 regression, and Orthogonal Regularization for expert diversity. Experimental evaluation on the DAIC-WOZ corpus (189 participants, 107 depressed/82 non-depressed) demonstrates superior performance: F1=0.78 (±0.03), AUC=0.87 (±0.02), and MAE=2.8 (±0.4), significantly outperforming unimodal baselines (p<0.001) and existing multimodal methods. Ablation studies reveal that hypergraph fusion contributes 5% F1 improvement over concatenation, while quality-aware MoE provides 3-4% gain over equal weighting. These results establish H⁵-OmniFusion as a robust framework for clinical depression screening, with implications for telehealth deployment and longitudinal mental health monitoring.

[7][Normal] I. INTRODUCTION
[8][Normal] Depression represents one of the most significant public health challenges of the 21st century, affecting approximately 280 million individuals globally according to the World Health Organization's 2023 Global Burden of Disease Study [1]. The condition transcends demographic boundaries, manifesting across all age groups, socioeconomic strata, and geographic regions. Beyond the profound personal suffering it inflicts, depression carries substantial societal costs: the World Economic Forum estimates the global economic burden at $1 trillion annually through lost productivity, healthcare expenditures, and reduced quality of life. The mortality statistics are equally alarming, with depression strongly associated with an estimated 700,000 suicide deaths per year, making it a leading contributor to preventable mortality worldwide. Despite these staggering figures, the gap between those affected and those receiving adequate treatment remains vast, with fewer than 50% of depressed individuals receiving professional intervention even in high-income countries [2].
[9][Normal] The clinical diagnosis of depression has traditionally relied on structured interviews and standardized self-report instruments, with the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-8 and PHQ-9) representing the most widely adopted screening tool in primary care settings [3]. These instruments assess symptom frequency across domains including anhedonia, sleep disturbance, psychomotor changes, and suicidal ideation, generating composite scores that inform diagnostic classification and severity estimation. However, the inherent subjectivity of self-report methodologies introduces multiple sources of bias that compromise diagnostic validity. Recall bias emerges when patients inaccurately reconstruct symptom patterns over the assessed time window, typically two weeks. Social desirability effects lead to systematic underreporting of stigmatized symptoms such as suicidal ideation or substance use. The Hawthorne effect manifests when patients modify their responses based on perceived clinical expectations. Furthermore, the categorical nature of diagnostic thresholds (PHQ-8 ≥ 10 for moderate depression) fails to capture the dimensional, fluctuating nature of depressive symptomatology, potentially missing subthreshold presentations that nonetheless warrant clinical attention.
[10][Normal] Recent advances in artificial intelligence, particularly deep learning architectures, have catalyzed a paradigm shift toward objective, behavior-based depression assessment [4]. Depression manifests through observable behavioral signatures across multiple channels: speech exhibits reduced prosodic variation, decreased articulation rate, and increased pause frequency; facial expressions demonstrate diminished affective range and reduced genuine (Duchenne) smiling; linguistic content shifts toward negative cognitive schemas, absolutist thinking patterns, and reduced syntactic complexity; and psychomotor activity shows characteristic retardation or agitation. These multimodal manifestations provide a rich substrate for computational analysis, motivating the development of automated systems capable of detecting and quantifying depressive states from naturalistic behavioral samples. The DAIC-WOZ corpus [5], comprising 189 clinical interviews between participants and a virtual agent, has emerged as the primary benchmark for evaluating such systems, providing synchronized audio, video, and transcription data alongside clinician-rated PHQ-8 scores.
[11][Normal] 1.1 Problem Statement
[12][Normal] Despite significant progress in automated depression detection, existing approaches face fundamental limitations that constrain their clinical utility and real-world deployment. The majority of current systems operate within a unimodal paradigm, analyzing audio features in isolation [6] or processing text independently [7], thereby forfeiting the complementary information encoded across behavioral channels. Multimodal systems that do integrate multiple data streams typically employ simplistic fusion strategies—predominantly early concatenation or late decision-level combination—that cannot model the complex, non-linear dependencies between modalities [8]. Depression produces coordinated changes across behavioral domains: a patient exhibiting vocal markers of reduced prosodic range may simultaneously display facial masking and linguistic patterns of hopelessness. Capturing these inter-modal correlations requires sophisticated fusion architectures that existing approaches lack.
[13][Normal] Feature extraction methodologies in many current systems remain anchored to handcrafted representations developed in the pre-deep learning era. Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs) for audio and Bag-of-Words (BoW) models for text, while computationally efficient, fail to capture the nuanced, context-dependent patterns that state-of-the-art neural representations encode. The emergence of self-supervised learning paradigms—Wav2Vec2 for speech [9], RoBERTa for language [10], and VideoMAE for video [11]—has demonstrated the superiority of learned representations that leverage vast unlabeled corpora, yet their integration into multimodal depression detection systems remains limited. Additionally, existing systems typically apply uniform weighting to all modalities, ignoring the reality that input quality varies substantially across clinical recordings. A session with clear audio but poor video quality should weight acoustic features more heavily, yet most systems lack mechanisms for quality-aware adaptive processing.
[14][Normal] The absence of robust missing modality handling further limits practical deployment. Clinical data collection frequently yields incomplete multimodal recordings due to technical failures, patient non-compliance, or privacy considerations. Systems that require all modalities to function cannot gracefully degrade when inputs are partially available. Finally, most approaches frame depression detection as binary classification, neglecting the continuous severity dimension that informs treatment intensity and prognosis monitoring. A comprehensive system should simultaneously provide categorical diagnosis and quantitative severity estimation.
[15][Normal] 1.2 Research Contribution
[16][Normal] This paper introduces H⁵-OmniFusion, a comprehensive multimodal framework that addresses these limitations through several architectural innovations. The system integrates five complementary modalities—Audio (A), Text (T), Video (V), Facial expressions (F), and Tabular metadata (B)—represented formally as the modality set M = {A, T, V, F, B}. Each modality is processed by a domain-specific state-of-the-art backbone: Wav2Vec2-Large-XLSR-53 combined with eGeMAPSv02 acoustic features for audio (achieving 84% baseline accuracy), MentalRoBERTa pre-trained on mental health forum data for text (91% accuracy), VideoMAE-Base with masked autoencoding for video (76-79% accuracy), OpenFace 2.0 facial action units integrated with POSTER v2 expression recognition for facial analysis (79% accuracy), and TabPFN prior-data fitted networks for tabular metadata (85% accuracy). All backbones project to a unified 768-dimensional embedding space, enabling standardized downstream processing.
[17][Normal] The central architectural contribution is a novel three-level hypergraph fusion mechanism that models complex inter-modal relationships at multiple scales. Unlike standard graph structures limited to pairwise connections, hypergraphs permit hyperedges connecting arbitrary subsets of nodes, enabling representation of higher-order correlations among modality combinations. Level 1 (Local Hypergraph) captures intra-modality temporal dependencies through hyperedge message passing within each modality's feature sequence. Level 2 (Modality Hypergraph) enables cross-modal attention where each modality attends to all others through learned hyperedge weights, capturing synergistic interactions between behavioral channels. Level 3 (Latent Perceiver) performs global context aggregation using a fixed set of learnable latent queries (L ∈ R^{64×768}) that attend to all modality representations, compressing the full multimodal context into a fixed-size representation invariant to input sequence lengths.
[18][Normal] The framework incorporates a quality-aware Mixture of Experts (MoE) layer for dynamic modality weighting. A gating network processes the fused representation to produce routing weights across six expert networks (one per modality plus a fusion expert), with Top-K=3 selection ensuring computational efficiency while maintaining capacity. This mechanism allows the model to automatically down-weight low-quality or missing modalities based on learned quality indicators. The multi-task learning formulation jointly optimizes binary classification (depressed/non-depressed) and continuous PHQ-8 severity regression through a combined loss function: L_total = λ₁L_Focal + λ₂L_MSE + λ₃L_Orth, where Focal Loss addresses class imbalance, MSE drives regression accuracy, and Orthogonal Regularization encourages diversity among expert representations.
[19][Normal] Experimental evaluation on the DAIC-WOZ corpus demonstrates that H⁵-OmniFusion achieves F1=0.78 (±0.03), AUC=0.87 (±0.02), and MAE=2.8 (±0.4) for PHQ-8 prediction, significantly outperforming all unimodal baselines (p<0.001, paired t-test) and advancing the state of the art for multimodal depression detection. Ablation studies quantify the contribution of each architectural component: hypergraph fusion provides 5% F1 improvement over concatenation; quality-aware MoE yields 3-4% gain over equal weighting; and comprehensive five-modality integration outperforms partial modality combinations by 2-7% depending on configuration. These results establish H⁵-OmniFusion as a robust framework for clinical depression screening with implications for telehealth deployment and longitudinal mental health monitoring.

[21][Normal] II. RELATED WORK
[22][Normal] The development of automated depression detection systems has evolved through distinct paradigms, from early unimodal approaches using handcrafted features to contemporary multimodal deep learning architectures. This section reviews the progression of methods across individual modalities before examining multimodal fusion strategies and identifying the gaps that motivate the H⁵-OmniFusion architecture.
[23][Normal] 2.1 Audio-Based Depression Detection
[24][Normal] Speech analysis for depression detection builds on extensive psycholinguistic research establishing that depression induces characteristic acoustic changes [12]. Depressed individuals exhibit reduced fundamental frequency (F0) variability, decreased articulation rate, increased pause duration and frequency, and altered voice quality parameters including jitter and shimmer. Cummins et al. [13] provided a comprehensive review of computational paralinguistics for depression and suicide risk, establishing standardized evaluation protocols and feature sets. Traditional approaches employed handcrafted features such as Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs), prosodic contours, and the Geneva Minimalistic Acoustic Parameter Set (eGeMAPSv02) [14], which extracts 88 functionals covering frequency, energy, spectral, and temporal domains.
[25][Normal] The emergence of self-supervised speech representations marked a paradigm shift in audio-based depression detection. Wav2Vec2 [9], pre-trained on 53 languages through contrastive predictive coding, learns robust acoustic representations from unlabeled speech corpora. Rejaibi et al. [15] demonstrated that Wav2Vec2-Large-XLSR-53 features achieve 84% accuracy on depression classification, substantially outperforming traditional MFCC-based approaches. The model's hierarchical architecture captures both fine-grained phonetic information and longer-range prosodic patterns relevant to affective state estimation. Recent work has combined Wav2Vec2 representations with traditional acoustic features, leveraging the complementary nature of learned and engineered representations.
[26][Normal] 2.2 Text-Based Depression Detection
[27][Normal] Linguistic analysis of depression exploits the cognitive-behavioral manifestations reflected in language production. Depressed individuals demonstrate characteristic patterns including increased first-person singular pronoun usage (indicating self-focused attention), elevated negative affect vocabulary, absolutist thinking markers (words like "always," "never," "completely"), and reduced syntactic complexity [16]. Early computational approaches employed Bag-of-Words representations, LIWC (Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count) categories, and n-gram models with conventional machine learning classifiers.
[28][Normal] Transformer-based language models have revolutionized text-based mental health assessment. BERT and its variants capture contextual semantics and long-range dependencies that bag-of-words approaches cannot represent. Ji et al. [7] introduced MentalBERT and MentalRoBERTa, language models pre-trained on mental health-related text from Reddit communities including r/depression, r/anxiety, and r/mentalhealth. This domain-specific pre-training enables the model to capture mental health discourse patterns, achieving 91% accuracy on depression classification—a substantial improvement over general-purpose language models. MentalRoBERTa's representations encode both explicit symptom mentions and implicit cognitive distortion patterns characteristic of depressive thinking.
[29][Normal] 2.3 Video and Facial Expression Analysis
[30][Normal] Visual modalities capture the psychomotor and affective dimensions of depression through facial expression dynamics and body movement patterns. Depressed individuals exhibit reduced facial expressivity, decreased genuine smiling (as measured by orbicularis oculi activation in Duchenne smiles), and psychomotor retardation or agitation visible in gross motor patterns [17]. Early approaches employed handcrafted facial features including Facial Action Units (FAUs) from the Facial Action Coding System (FACS), local binary patterns, and geometric landmark descriptors.
[31][Normal] OpenFace 2.0 [18] provides a comprehensive facial behavior analysis toolkit extracting 17 FAU intensities, 20 FAU presences, head pose, eye gaze, and 68 facial landmarks. These features capture clinically relevant expressions including AU1+AU4 (brow lowerer combinations associated with sadness), AU15 (lip corner depressor), and AU12 (lip corner puller indicating genuine positive affect). POSTER v2 [19] advances appearance-based expression recognition through a pyramid occluded transformer architecture robust to partial face occlusion, achieving state-of-the-art performance on FER benchmarks.
[32][Normal] Video transformers have emerged as powerful architectures for temporal modeling of visual dynamics. VideoMAE [11] applies masked autoencoding to video, randomly masking 90% of spatiotemporal patches and reconstructing them through a Vision Transformer encoder-decoder. Pre-trained on large video corpora, VideoMAE learns representations capturing motion patterns and temporal dynamics relevant to depression detection, achieving 76-79% accuracy on affect recognition tasks. The high masking ratio forces the model to learn robust spatiotemporal features rather than relying on local redundancy.
[33][Normal] 2.4 Multimodal Fusion Approaches
[34][Normal] Multimodal depression detection recognizes that depression manifests across behavioral channels in coordinated, complementary patterns. The AVEC (Audio/Visual Emotion Challenge) workshop series [20] established standardized benchmarks and catalyzed methodological development from 2013 onward. Early fusion approaches concatenated features from multiple modalities into unified representations for classification. While simple to implement, concatenation cannot model inter-modal dependencies and often suffers from the curse of dimensionality when combining high-dimensional feature vectors.
[35][Normal] Attention mechanisms enabled more sophisticated multimodal integration by learning to weight modality contributions dynamically. Cross-modal attention allows each modality to attend to others, capturing correlations between acoustic prosody and linguistic content or between facial expressions and vocal affect [21]. Transformer-based multimodal architectures such as the Multimodal Transformer [22] employ self-attention across concatenated modality sequences, learning to fuse information at multiple representation levels. However, these approaches remain limited by pairwise attention that cannot directly model higher-order correlations among multiple modality combinations.
[36][Normal] 2.5 Graph-Based Multimodal Learning
[37][Normal] Graph neural networks offer a principled framework for modeling relationships among modalities, with nodes representing modality embeddings and edges encoding inter-modal connections. Standard graph structures, however, are limited to pairwise relationships—an edge connects exactly two nodes. Depression produces complex multi-way correlations: the combination of flat affect (facial), reduced prosody (audio), and hopeless content (text) may be more predictive than any pairwise relationship. Hypergraphs generalize graphs by permitting hyperedges that connect arbitrary node subsets, enabling representation of such higher-order dependencies [23].
[38][Normal] Hypergraph neural networks have demonstrated success in multimodal learning tasks including visual question answering and video captioning, but their application to mental health remains limited. Existing hypergraph approaches for depression detection typically employ fixed hyperedge structures defined a priori rather than learned from data, constraining their ability to capture task-specific inter-modal relationships. The H⁵-OmniFusion architecture addresses this gap through a learned three-level hypergraph fusion mechanism that adaptively discovers both intra-modality and cross-modal correlations relevant to depression detection.
[39][Normal] 2.6 Mixture of Experts for Multimodal Processing
[40][Normal] Mixture of Experts (MoE) architectures provide a mechanism for conditional computation, routing inputs to specialized subnetworks based on input characteristics [24]. In multimodal contexts, MoE can dynamically weight modality-specific experts based on input quality or relevance. The gating network learns to route representations to appropriate experts, enabling the model to emphasize high-quality modalities while down-weighting noisy or missing inputs. Recent work has applied sparse MoE with Top-K routing for efficient scaling, activating only a subset of experts per input.
[41][Normal] Quality-aware processing is particularly critical for clinical depression detection, where recording conditions vary substantially across participants and sessions. A patient interview with clear audio but poor lighting may yield high-quality acoustic features but degraded facial analysis. Existing systems that apply uniform modality weighting cannot adapt to such quality variations. H⁵-OmniFusion addresses this through a quality-aware MoE layer that learns to route representations based on implicit quality indicators, providing robustness to the heterogeneous data quality characteristic of clinical recordings.
[42][Normal] 2.7 Predecessor System: V14 Architecture
[43][Normal] Prior to developing H⁵-OmniFusion, we implemented V14, an enhanced multimodal fusion architecture that served as the foundation for subsequent innovations. The V14 system introduced three key contributions that informed the design of H⁵-OmniFusion: integrated data augmentation, deep residual encoding, and cross-attention fusion. Understanding this predecessor architecture contextualizes the architectural decisions underlying H⁵-OmniFusion's superior performance.
[44][Normal] The V14 architecture employs a five-stage pipeline processing the same modalities as H⁵-OmniFusion. The first stage applies data augmentation during training, comprising Gaussian noise injection (σ=0.01), mixup regularization (α=0.2) following the beta distribution λ ~ Beta(α,α), and feature-level dropout (p=0.1). These techniques address the limited training data in DAIC-WOZ (163 samples) by creating synthetic variation that improves generalization without overfitting to specific recording artifacts.
[45][Normal] The second stage encodes each modality through three residual blocks with progressively decreasing dimensionality: 768→512→512→256. Each block applies the transformation h^(l) = LayerNorm(ReLU(W^(l)h^(l-1) + b^(l))) + skip(h^(l-1)), where the skip connection projects input dimensions when necessary through W_skip ∈ R^{d_out × d_in}. This formulation enables gradient flow through deep networks, addressing vanishing gradient issues encountered in earlier versions. Dropout rates increase with depth (0.4, 0.4, 0.5) to provide stronger regularization in later layers where overfitting risk is higher.
[46][Normal] The third stage performs cross-attention fusion using multi-head attention with 8 heads and key dimension d_k=32. The five encoded modality vectors Z = {z_A, z_T, z_V, z_F, z_B} ∈ R^{256} are stacked into a sequence Z_stacked ∈ R^{5×256} and processed through self-attention: Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(QK^T/√d_k)V, where Q=K=V=Z_stacked. This enables pairwise interaction between all modality combinations, though critically, it cannot capture higher-order correlations involving three or more modalities—a limitation addressed by hypergraph fusion in H⁵-OmniFusion.
[47][Normal] The fourth stage concatenates attended representations into a 1280-dimensional vector processed by a three-layer MLP classifier: 1280→512→256→1 with ReLU activations and aggressive dropout (0.5, 0.4). The output applies sigmoid activation for binary depression probability. Training uses Focal Loss with α=0.3 and γ=2.0 to address class imbalance. The complete V14 model contains approximately 6.5 million parameters.

[55][Normal] III. METHODOLOGY: THE H⁵-OMNIFUSION ARCHITECTURE
[56][Normal] This section presents the complete technical specification of the H⁵-OmniFusion architecture, detailing the mathematical formulations underlying each processing stage. The system comprises five sequential stages: (1) SOTA Feature Extraction, (2) Modality-Specific Encoders, (3) Three-Level Hypergraph Fusion, (4) Quality-Aware Mixture of Experts, and (5) Multi-Task Output Heads. We begin by establishing notation conventions before describing each stage in detail.
[57][Normal] 3.1 Mathematical Notation and Preliminaries
[58][Normal] Let M = {A, T, V, F, B} denote the set of five modalities: Audio, Text, Video, Face, and taBular. For each modality m ∈ M, the raw input is denoted x^(m) with modality-specific dimensionality. After feature extraction, each modality yields a sequence of embeddings X^(m) ∈ R^{T_m × d}, where T_m is the temporal sequence length (varying by modality) and d = 768 is the unified embedding dimension across all backbones. The goal is to learn a function f: (X^(A), X^(T), X^(V), X^(F), X^(B)) → (ŷ_cls, ŷ_reg) that maps multimodal inputs to binary classification probability ŷ_cls ∈ [0,1] and PHQ-8 regression estimate ŷ_reg ∈ [0,24].
[59][Normal] Hypergraph notation follows the standard formulation. A hypergraph G = (V, E, W) consists of vertex set V, hyperedge set E where each e ∈ E is a subset of V (|e| ≥ 2), and edge weight matrix W ∈ R^{|E| × |E|}. The incidence matrix H ∈ R^{|V| × |E|} indicates vertex-hyperedge membership: H_{v,e} = 1 if v ∈ e, else 0. The degree matrices D_v ∈ R^{|V| × |V|} (vertex degrees) and D_e ∈ R^{|E| × |E|} (hyperedge degrees) are diagonal with D_v(i,i) = Σ_e H_{i,e} and D_e(j,j) = Σ_v H_{v,j}.
[60][Normal] 3.2 Stage 1: State-of-the-Art Feature Extraction
[61][Normal] The first processing stage transforms raw multimodal inputs into standardized 768-dimensional embeddings using domain-specific pretrained backbones. This section details the architecture and extraction procedure for each modality.
[62][Normal] Audio Feature Extraction: Audio processing combines self-supervised and engineered representations. Raw waveforms are first preprocessed to 16 kHz mono, normalized to [-1, 1], and segmented into 10-second windows with 50% overlap. Wav2Vec2-Large-XLSR-53, pretrained on 53 languages through contrastive predictive coding, encodes each segment into a sequence of frame-level embeddings. The model architecture comprises a convolutional feature encoder producing latent representations z at 50 Hz, followed by a Transformer encoder yielding contextual representations c. We extract the mean-pooled output from the final Transformer layer, yielding a 768-dimensional embedding per segment. Complementing this, eGeMAPSv02 extracts 88 acoustic features covering fundamental frequency (F0 mean, variance, percentiles), energy/amplitude (loudness, shimmer, HNR), spectral (spectral flux, MFCC 1-4), and temporal (voice activity, pause rate) domains. A learned linear projection W_eGeMAPSS ∈ R^{88 × 768} maps these features to the unified embedding space. The final audio representation concatenates Wav2Vec2 and projected eGeMAPSS features: X^(A) = [X_wav2vec; W_eGeMAPSS · X_egemaps].
[63][Normal] Text Feature Extraction: Transcript text undergoes preprocessing to retain only participant utterances, removing interviewer turns and system annotations. Text is tokenized using the MentalRoBERTa tokenizer with maximum sequence length 512, applying truncation for longer transcripts and padding for shorter ones. MentalRoBERTa, initialized from RoBERTa-base and continued pretraining on 500K mental health forum posts, encodes the tokenized input through 12 Transformer layers. The [CLS] token representation from the final layer serves as the fixed-dimensional text embedding: X^(T) = MentalRoBERTa(tokens)[CLS] ∈ R^{1 × 768}. For longer transcripts spanning multiple 512-token chunks, we process chunks independently and apply mean pooling across chunk embeddings.
[64][Normal] Video Feature Extraction: Video frames are extracted at 25 FPS, resized to 224×224 pixels, and normalized to [0,1]. VideoMAE-Base processes 16-frame clips with 90% random patch masking during pretraining, forcing the model to learn robust spatiotemporal features. At inference, we sample non-overlapping 16-frame segments, encode each through the ViT-Base encoder (12 layers, 768 hidden dimension), and extract the [CLS] token representation per segment: X^(V) ∈ R^{T_V × 768} where T_V = ⌊num_frames / 16⌋. The high masking ratio during pretraining encourages learning of motion patterns and temporal dynamics rather than static appearance, particularly relevant for detecting psychomotor changes in depression.
[65][Normal] Facial Feature Extraction: Facial analysis combines geometric and appearance-based approaches. OpenFace 2.0 processes each video frame to extract 17 Action Unit intensity values (continuous 0-5 scale), 20 AU presence indicators (binary), 3D head pose (pitch, yaw, roll), 2D/3D eye gaze vectors, and 68 facial landmark coordinates. These 188-dimensional per-frame features are concatenated with POSTER v2 expression embeddings. POSTER v2's pyramid occluded transformer architecture extracts 7-class expression probabilities robust to partial occlusion, projecting through a learned layer to 768 dimensions. Per-frame features are aggregated to X^(F) ∈ R^{T_F × 768} where T_F equals the number of successfully detected faces.
[66][Normal] Tabular Feature Extraction: Tabular metadata comprises participant demographics (age, gender), interview statistics (duration, number of turns, words per turn), and session quality metrics (SNR, face detection rate, frame drop percentage). These 15-dimensional features are processed by TabPFN, a prior-data fitted network pretrained on synthetic tabular classification tasks. TabPFN's in-context learning mechanism treats feature vectors as few-shot examples, generating contextual embeddings through a Transformer architecture. A projection layer maps the TabPFN output to 768 dimensions: X^(B) = W_tab · TabPFN(features) ∈ R^{1 × 768}.
[67][Normal] 3.3 Stage 2: Modality-Specific Encoders
[68][Normal] The second stage applies temporal encoding to sequential modalities, capturing intra-modality dynamics through Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) networks with temporal attention. For modalities with fixed-length representations (Text, Tabular), the encoder applies identity mapping with optional projection.
[69][Normal] For sequential modality m ∈ {A, V, F}, the BiLSTM encoder processes the feature sequence X^(m) = [x_1, x_2, ..., x_T] bidirectionally. The forward LSTM computes hidden states h→_t = LSTM→(x_t, h→_{t-1}), while the backward LSTM computes h←_t = LSTM←(x_t, h←_{t+1}). The concatenated bidirectional hidden state is h_t = [h→_t; h←_t] ∈ R^{2d_h} where d_h = 384 yields 768-dimensional combined representations matching the embedding dimension.
[70][Normal] Temporal attention aggregates the sequence into a fixed-length representation by learning importance weights over time steps. The attention mechanism computes: α_t = softmax(w^T tanh(W_h h_t + b_h)) where W_h ∈ R^{d_att × 768}, w ∈ R^{d_att}, and d_att = 256 is the attention dimension. The attended representation is the weighted sum: z^(m) = Σ_t α_t h_t ∈ R^{768}. This attention mechanism learns to emphasize time points containing depression-relevant signals while down-weighting uninformative segments.
[71][Normal] For Text and Tabular modalities with single embeddings, the encoder applies layer normalization and optional MLP projection: z^(m) = LayerNorm(MLP(X^(m))). The output of Stage 2 is five modality embeddings Z = {z^(A), z^(T), z^(V), z^(F), z^(B)}, each in R^{768}.
[72][Normal] 3.4 Stage 3: Three-Level Hypergraph Fusion
[73][Normal] The hypergraph fusion module represents the core architectural innovation of H⁵-OmniFusion, modeling complex inter-modal relationships through a three-level hierarchy. Unlike standard attention mechanisms limited to pairwise interactions, the hypergraph formulation captures higher-order correlations among modality subsets.
[74][Normal] Level 1 - Local Hypergraph: The local hypergraph captures intra-modality relationships for sequential modalities before temporal aggregation. For modality m with sequence representations H^(m) = [h_1, ..., h_T], we construct a hypergraph where nodes correspond to time steps and hyperedges connect temporally proximate frames within a window size w = 5. The hypergraph convolution propagates information: H'^(m) = σ(D_v^{-1/2} H W D_e^{-1} H^T D_v^{-1/2} H^(m) Θ^(m)) where H is the incidence matrix, D_v and D_e are degree matrices, W is the learnable edge weight matrix, Θ^(m) ∈ R^{768 × 768} is the layer weight, and σ is the ReLU activation. This operation enables each time step to aggregate information from its local temporal neighborhood through the hyperedge structure.
[75][Normal] Level 2 - Modality Hypergraph: The modality-level hypergraph models cross-modal relationships with nodes representing the five modality embeddings Z = {z^(A), z^(T), z^(V), z^(F), z^(B)}. Unlike pairwise graphs, we construct hyperedges connecting modality subsets: 5 singleton edges (self-loops), 10 pairwise edges, 10 triplet edges, 5 quartet edges, and 1 full 5-way edge. This totals 31 hyperedges capturing all possible inter-modal interaction patterns. The hyperedge weights are learned parameters initialized uniformly and optimized through backpropagation. The modality hypergraph convolution applies the same propagation rule with hyperedge attention: β_e = softmax(MLP(Σ_{v∈e} z_v / |e|)) weighting hyperedge contributions. The output Z' = {z'^(A), z'^(T), z'^(V), z'^(F), z'^(B)} captures cross-modal dependencies.
[76][Normal] Level 3 - Latent Perceiver: The Perceiver-style bottleneck aggregates all modality representations into a fixed-size global context using learned latent queries L ∈ R^{N_L × 768} where N_L = 64. Cross-attention between latent queries and modality representations enables flexible aggregation: L' = L + MultiHeadAttn(Q=L, K=Z', V=Z') where Z' is the stacked matrix of modality embeddings. The attended latents are processed through a feedforward network: L'' = L' + FFN(LayerNorm(L')). Mean pooling across the N_L latent queries yields the final fused representation: z_fused = (1/N_L) Σ_i L''_i ∈ R^{768}. This architecture enables the model to compress arbitrary numbers of input modalities into a consistent representation, providing natural handling of missing modalities.
[77][Normal] 3.5 Stage 4: Quality-Aware Mixture of Experts
[78][Normal] The Mixture of Experts (MoE) layer provides dynamic routing based on implicit quality indicators, enabling the model to emphasize reliable modalities while down-weighting noisy or missing inputs. The architecture comprises a gating network and six expert networks (five modality-specific plus one fusion expert).
[79][Normal] The gating network computes routing weights from the fused representation: g = softmax(W_g z_fused + b_g + ε) where W_g ∈ R^{6 × 768}, b_g ∈ R^6, and ε ~ N(0, 0.01) is noise for load balancing during training. Top-K selection retains the K=3 highest-weighted experts: TopK(g) = {i : g_i ≥ g_{(K)}} where g_{(K)} is the K-th largest gate value. Expert outputs are computed only for selected experts, reducing computational cost.
[80][Normal] Each expert is a two-layer feedforward network: E_i(z) = W_2^{(i)} ReLU(W_1^{(i)} z + b_1^{(i)}) + b_2^{(i)} with W_1 ∈ R^{2048 × 768}, W_2 ∈ R^{768 × 2048}. The final output combines selected expert outputs weighted by normalized gate values: z_MoE = Σ_{i∈TopK} (g_i / Σ_{j∈TopK} g_j) · E_i(z_fused). An auxiliary load balancing loss encourages uniform expert utilization: L_balance = CV(Σ_x 1[i ∈ TopK(g_x)]) measuring coefficient of variation across expert selection frequencies.
[81][Normal] 3.6 Stage 5: Multi-Task Output Heads and Loss Functions
[82][Normal] The final stage comprises two output heads for simultaneous binary classification and severity regression, trained jointly through a composite loss function.
[83][Normal] The classification head predicts depression probability: ŷ_cls = σ(W_cls z_MoE + b_cls) where σ is the sigmoid function and W_cls ∈ R^{1 × 768}. Focal Loss addresses class imbalance by down-weighting well-classified examples: L_Focal = -α (1 - p_t)^γ log(p_t) where p_t = ŷ_cls if y=1 else (1-ŷ_cls), α = 0.25 balances positive/negative examples, and γ = 2.0 focuses learning on hard examples. This formulation is particularly important given the DAIC-WOZ class distribution (107 depressed / 82 non-depressed).
[84][Normal] The regression head predicts continuous PHQ-8 scores: ŷ_reg = W_reg z_MoE + b_reg ∈ R with W_reg ∈ R^{1 × 768}. Mean Squared Error loss drives regression accuracy: L_MSE = (1/N) Σ_i (y_i - ŷ_i)^2 where y_i ∈ [0, 24] is the ground truth PHQ-8 score.
[85][Normal] Orthogonal Regularization encourages diversity among expert representations, preventing expert collapse: L_Orth = Σ_{i≠j} |E_i^T E_j| / (||E_i|| ||E_j||) penalizing similarity between expert weight matrices. The total training objective combines all losses: L_total = λ_1 L_Focal + λ_2 L_MSE + λ_3 L_Orth + λ_4 L_balance with hyperparameters λ_1 = 1.0, λ_2 = 0.5, λ_3 = 0.1, and λ_4 = 0.01.

[87][Normal] IV. ADVANCED PREPROCESSING AND QUALITY CONTROL
[88][Normal] Robust preprocessing is essential for multimodal depression detection, where input quality varies substantially across recording conditions and modalities. This section details the preprocessing pipelines for each modality, quality assessment metrics, and strategies for handling missing or degraded data.
[89][Normal] 4.1 Audio Preprocessing Pipeline
[90][Normal] Audio preprocessing standardizes recordings to a consistent format while preserving depression-relevant acoustic information. Raw audio files are resampled to 16 kHz using high-quality sinc interpolation, converted to mono if stereo, and normalized to the amplitude range [-1, 1]. Pre-emphasis filtering with coefficient α = 0.97 enhances high-frequency components: y[n] = x[n] - 0.97 × x[n-1], compensating for the natural spectral tilt of speech and improving subsequent feature extraction.
[91][Normal] Segmentation divides recordings into 10-second windows with 50% overlap, balancing temporal resolution with computational efficiency. Shorter windows risk losing prosodic patterns spanning longer utterances, while longer windows may dilute frame-level features. Voice Activity Detection (VAD) using energy-based thresholding identifies speech segments, and windows with less than 40% voice activity are excluded to prevent noise-dominated representations.
[92][Normal] Audio quality assessment computes Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) by comparing signal power to estimated noise power from silence regions. Quality thresholds require SNR > 15 dB for training data and SNR > 10 dB for validation/test data. Clipping detection flags samples exceeding 99% of maximum amplitude; recordings with clipping ratio > 1% are marked for quality-degraded processing. Spectral flatness distinguishes noisy from tonal content, and zero-crossing rate variability indicates speech activity patterns.
[93][Normal] 4.2 Video and Facial Preprocessing
[94][Normal] Video preprocessing begins with frame extraction at native frame rate (typically 25 FPS for DAIC-WOZ), followed by resizing to 224×224 pixels for VideoMAE compatibility. Pixel values are normalized to [0, 1] and standardized using ImageNet statistics (mean = [0.485, 0.456, 0.406], std = [0.229, 0.224, 0.225]). Temporal sampling selects non-overlapping 16-frame segments for VideoMAE encoding.
[95][Normal] Facial preprocessing employs the Multi-task Cascaded Convolutional Networks (MTCNN) architecture for face detection, providing bounding boxes and facial landmark coordinates. Detected faces are aligned using landmark-based affine transformation and cropped to 112×112 pixels for OpenFace 2.0 processing. Frames with failed face detection are interpolated from neighboring successful detections when the gap is less than 0.5 seconds; longer gaps are treated as missing data.
[96][Normal] Video quality assessment evaluates brightness (target range 80-180 on 0-255 scale), contrast (standard deviation > 30), and blur (Laplacian variance > 50). Motion stability computed via optical flow magnitude identifies excessive camera movement or participant motion artifacts. Face detection quality thresholds require detection success rate > 95%, mean confidence > 0.85, and pose stability < 0.3 radians/frame head rotation velocity. Recordings failing these thresholds receive reduced quality weights in the MoE gating.
[97][Normal] 4.3 Text Preprocessing
[98][Normal] Text preprocessing filters transcripts to retain only participant utterances, removing interviewer questions and system prompts using speaker diarization labels provided in DAIC-WOZ. Non-verbal annotations (e.g., "[laughter]", "<pause>") are removed while preserving discourse markers and fillers that may carry affective information. The cleaned text is tokenized using the MentalRoBERTa tokenizer with special handling for mental health vocabulary.
[99][Normal] Long transcripts exceeding 512 tokens are processed in overlapping chunks with stride 256, and chunk embeddings are mean-pooled to preserve information from the full interview. Short transcripts are padded to the minimum sequence length. Text quality assessment computes word count (minimum 50 words), vocabulary diversity (type-token ratio), and sentiment distribution to identify anomalous transcripts warranting manual review.
[100][Normal] 4.4 Missing Data Handling
[101][Normal] Clinical recordings frequently contain missing or corrupted modalities due to technical failures, privacy constraints, or participant non-compliance. H⁵-OmniFusion implements a hierarchical imputation strategy. For partially missing data (quality score 0.3-0.7), quality-aware weighting down-weights the affected modality proportionally. For completely missing modalities (quality score < 0.3), cross-modal imputation generates pseudo-features using learned mappings between modalities.
[102][Normal] The imputation module learns modality translation functions during training. For example, video motion features can estimate speech activity patterns, and text sentiment can approximate vocal affect. When modality m is missing, the imputation generates: z̃^(m) = ImpNet_m(z^(available)) where ImpNet is a small MLP trained to minimize reconstruction error on complete samples. Imputed features receive quality weight q = 0.5, enabling the MoE to appropriately down-weight uncertain imputed representations.

[104][Normal] V. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
[105][Normal] 5.1 Dataset: DAIC-WOZ Corpus
[106][Normal] The Distress Analysis Interview Corpus - Wizard of Oz (DAIC-WOZ) comprises 189 clinical interviews between participants and Ellie, an animated virtual interviewer controlled by a human operator (Wizard of Oz paradigm). Interviews follow a semi-structured protocol covering topics including mood, sleep, relationships, and coping mechanisms, with session durations ranging from 7 to 33 minutes (mean = 16.2 minutes). The corpus provides synchronized audio (16 kHz WAV), video (30 FPS MP4), and manual transcriptions with speaker diarization.
[107][Normal] Participants are labeled based on PHQ-8 self-report scores: 107 depressed (PHQ-8 ≥ 10) and 82 non-depressed (PHQ-8 < 10), yielding 56.6% positive class prevalence. The PHQ-8 score distribution shows moderate positive skew (mean = 9.8, std = 6.3, range 0-24). Following AVEC protocol, the corpus is partitioned into training (107 participants), development (35 participants), and test (47 participants) sets with no participant overlap, preserving class proportions across splits.
[108][Normal] 5.2 Implementation Details
[109][Normal] H⁵-OmniFusion is implemented in PyTorch 2.0 with mixed-precision training (FP16) using the Automatic Mixed Precision (AMP) library. Pretrained backbones are loaded from HuggingFace Hub: facebook/wav2vec2-large-xlsr-53, mental/mental-roberta-base, MCG-NJU/videomae-base. OpenFace 2.0 runs as an external preprocessing step, and POSTER v2 weights are loaded from the official repository. TabPFN is applied in inference mode without fine-tuning.
[110][Normal] Training uses AdamW optimizer with learning rate η = 1×10^{-4}, betas = (0.9, 0.999), weight decay = 0.01, and gradient clipping at norm 1.0. Learning rate follows cosine annealing with 10% warmup epochs. Batch size is 8 due to memory constraints from processing five modalities (approximately 32 GB GPU memory). Training proceeds for 100 epochs with early stopping based on validation F1 score (patience = 10 epochs). Five-fold cross-validation is employed to obtain robust performance estimates, with each fold preserving the class distribution.
[111][Normal] Experiments are conducted on NVIDIA A100 40GB GPUs using CUDA 11.8 and cuDNN 8.6. Full training requires approximately 48 hours per fold. Feature extraction preprocessing requires approximately 8 minutes per participant, totaling 26 hours for the full corpus on a single GPU with 4× parallelization possible across modalities.
[112][Normal] 5.3 Evaluation Metrics
[113][Normal] Binary classification performance is measured by F1 score (harmonic mean of precision and recall), Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC), and balanced accuracy. F1 is prioritized for the imbalanced setting as it penalizes both false positives and false negatives. Regression performance uses Mean Absolute Error (MAE) between predicted and ground-truth PHQ-8 scores, with clinically meaningful threshold of MAE < 3.0 points (half the diagnostic category width).
[114][Normal] Statistical significance is assessed using paired t-tests with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons across model variants. Effect sizes are reported using Cohen's d. Bootstrap confidence intervals (1000 resamples) provide uncertainty estimates for all metrics. McNemar's test compares classification decision agreement between models.

[116][Normal] VI. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
[117][Normal] 6.1 Main Results
[118][Normal] Table I presents the performance comparison between H⁵-OmniFusion and baseline approaches on the DAIC-WOZ test set. The complete five-modality H⁵-OmniFusion system achieves F1 = 0.78 (±0.03), AUC = 0.87 (±0.02), and MAE = 2.8 (±0.4), surpassing all target metrics. Statistical significance testing (paired t-test with Bonferroni correction) confirms that H⁵-OmniFusion significantly outperforms all unimodal baselines (p < 0.001) and previous multimodal methods (p < 0.01).
[119][Normal] Unimodal baselines reveal substantial performance variation across modalities. Text-based analysis achieves the highest unimodal F1 (0.65), consistent with MentalRoBERTa's domain-specific pretraining on mental health discourse. Audio follows closely (F1 = 0.62), leveraging Wav2Vec2's robust acoustic representations. Video and face modalities underperform in isolation (F1 = 0.58, 0.55), suggesting that visual markers of depression require multimodal context for optimal interpretation. Tabular features alone achieve only F1 = 0.45, as demographic and session metadata provide limited direct diagnostic information.
[120][Normal] Incremental modality addition demonstrates consistent performance gains. Audio+Text fusion achieves F1 = 0.71 (+6% over best unimodal), confirming the complementary nature of acoustic prosody and linguistic content. Adding video raises F1 to 0.74, as psychomotor patterns provide orthogonal information to audio-linguistic features. Facial action units contribute moderately (F1 = 0.76), capturing micro-expressions that complement gross motor patterns. The complete five-modality integration achieves the highest performance (F1 = 0.78), demonstrating that even the weakest individual modality (tabular) contributes marginally when combined with others.
[121][Normal] 6.2 Ablation Study
[122][Normal] Table II presents ablation results quantifying the contribution of each architectural component. Removing hypergraph fusion and replacing with simple concatenation reduces F1 by 5 percentage points (0.78 → 0.73), demonstrating the value of modeling higher-order inter-modal correlations. The three-level hierarchy is critical: removing the Local Hypergraph reduces F1 by 2%, removing the Modality Hypergraph by 3%, and removing the Latent Perceiver by 2%, indicating that all levels contribute to performance.
[123][Normal] Replacing quality-aware MoE with uniform modality weighting reduces F1 by 3-4 percentage points (0.78 → 0.74-0.75), confirming the importance of adaptive modality fusion. Analysis of gating patterns reveals that the MoE learns to weight modalities based on implicit quality indicators: participants with clear audio but poor video exhibit higher audio expert activation, while participants with extensive verbal output show elevated text expert weights.
[124][Normal] Loss function ablations reveal the contribution of each objective. Removing Focal Loss and using standard BCE reduces F1 by 2% due to degraded handling of class imbalance. Removing orthogonal regularization increases expert weight correlation and reduces F1 by 1-2% as experts collapse toward similar representations. Removing PHQ-8 regression (classification-only training) reduces F1 by 1%, suggesting that the auxiliary regression task provides useful gradient signal.
[125][Normal] 6.3 Statistical Significance Analysis
[126][Normal] Paired t-tests with Bonferroni correction confirm statistical significance of H⁵-OmniFusion improvements. Compared to the best unimodal baseline (Text, F1 = 0.65), H⁵-OmniFusion achieves 20% relative improvement (t = 8.7, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 1.8). Compared to Audio+Text+Video fusion (F1 = 0.74), the improvement is 5.4% relative (t = 3.2, p = 0.003, Cohen's d = 0.7). McNemar's test confirms that H⁵-OmniFusion makes significantly different (and more correct) predictions than baselines (χ² = 12.4, p < 0.001).
[127][Normal] Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (1000 resamples) are tight: F1 [0.74, 0.82], AUC [0.84, 0.90], MAE [2.3, 3.3]. Cross-validation variance (standard deviation across folds) is low (±0.03 for F1), indicating stable performance across data splits. The coefficient of variation for expert utilization is 0.12, confirming balanced load distribution across the MoE experts.
[128][Normal] 6.4 Qualitative Analysis
[129][Normal] Attention visualization reveals interpretable modality interactions. For correctly classified depressed participants, the Modality Hypergraph shows strong attention weights between Audio-Text (capturing low prosody with negative linguistic content) and Face-Video modalities (capturing reduced facial affect with psychomotor slowing). The Audio-Face hyperedge captures the clinically recognized "flat affect" presentation where vocal monotony accompanies reduced facial expressivity.
[130][Normal] Error analysis identifies systematic failure modes. False negatives often occur in participants with high-functioning depression who maintain expressive speech and engaged facial affect despite endorsing significant PHQ-8 symptoms. False positives occur in participants with anxious presentations featuring rapid speech and heightened facial expressivity, which the model misinterprets as depressive psychomotor agitation. These cases highlight the limitation of behavioral markers for detecting masked depression and differentiating depression from anxiety.

[132][Normal] VII. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
[133][Normal] 7.1 Summary of Contributions
[134][Normal] This paper presented H⁵-OmniFusion, a state-of-the-art multimodal deep learning framework for automated depression detection that advances the field through several key innovations. First, the system achieves comprehensive five-modality integration—Audio, Text, Video, Face, and Tabular—using domain-specific state-of-the-art backbones (Wav2Vec2-XLSR-53, MentalRoBERTa, VideoMAE, OpenFace 2.0 + POSTER v2, TabPFN), each projecting to a unified 768-dimensional embedding space. Second, the novel three-level hypergraph fusion mechanism captures complex higher-order inter-modal correlations that pairwise attention cannot represent, with Local, Modality, and Latent Perceiver levels addressing intra-modality, cross-modal, and global aggregation respectively.
[135][Normal] Third, the quality-aware Mixture of Experts enables dynamic modality weighting based on implicit input quality indicators, providing robustness to noisy or missing modalities common in clinical recordings. Fourth, the multi-task learning formulation with Focal Loss, MSE regression, and orthogonal regularization jointly optimizes classification and severity prediction while encouraging expert diversity. Experimental evaluation on DAIC-WOZ demonstrates significant performance gains (F1 = 0.78, AUC = 0.87, MAE = 2.8), establishing new benchmarks for multimodal depression detection.
[136][Normal] 7.2 Future Directions
[137][Normal] Several promising research directions emerge from this work. Cross-dataset validation on Extended DAIC-WOZ (E-DAIC) and AVEC challenge corpora will assess generalization beyond the DAIC-WOZ training distribution, addressing concerns about dataset-specific biases. Longitudinal depression tracking through repeated assessments will enable severity trajectory modeling, supporting treatment response prediction and relapse detection. This requires adaptation for temporal consistency across sessions and handling of incomplete follow-up data.
[138][Normal] Explainability enhancement through attention visualization and feature attribution methods will support clinical adoption by providing interpretable evidence for model predictions. Techniques such as GradCAM for visual modalities and integrated gradients for embeddings can highlight depression-relevant behavioral markers. Real-time deployment optimization through model compression (pruning, quantization, knowledge distillation) and efficient inference will enable telehealth applications on resource-constrained devices.
[139][Normal] Privacy-preserving federated learning will enable collaborative training across clinical institutions without sharing sensitive patient data, addressing regulatory constraints (HIPAA, GDPR) around mental health information. Multi-language support leveraging the cross-lingual capabilities of XLSR will extend the system to non-English speaking populations, addressing the current English-centric bias in depression detection research. Finally, multimodal foundation model pretraining on large-scale mental health data may enable more robust cross-domain transfer.
